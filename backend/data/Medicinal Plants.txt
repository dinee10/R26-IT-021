Name (Sinhala): හතාවාරිය / Hathawariya
Scientific Name: Asparagus racemosus
Family Name: Asparagaceae
Medicinal Usage: Hathawariya is a well-known female reproductive tonic. It is used to improve milk production in nursing mothers, treat menstrual disorders, and address infertility. It also has anti-ulcer, antioxidant, and immune-boosting properties. It soothes burning sensations and is used for gastric ulcers.
Plant Part Used: Roots
Preparation: The dried roots are ground into powder and taken with warm milk or water. A herbal jam (s chyavanaprasha) contains Hathawariya. For nursing mothers, root decoction is given twice daily to enhance breast milk.
Precautions/Limitations: Has estrogen-like effects, so it should be used cautiously by individuals with hormone-sensitive conditions; pregnant or breastfeeding women should consult a doctor before regular use.
Scientific Evidence Level: Preliminary clinical evidence for milk production (few small randomized trials); other uses, including anti-ulcer, are traditional or preclinical.
Evidence by Use: Increasing breast milk: two small randomized placebo-controlled trials in lactating mothers (main outcome: prolactin level); one reported a more than three-fold prolactin rise versus control. Evidence base is small. Menstrual disorders, infertility, anti-ulcer: traditional use.

Name (English): Venivel / Calumba
Name (Sinhala): වෙනිවැල් / 
Scientific Name: Coscinium fenestratum
Family Name: Menispermaceae
Medicinal Usage: Venivel is a very bitter herb used for diabetes, fever, jaundice, and skin diseases. It has strong anti-diabetic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-malarial properties. It is also used to treat peptic ulcers, dysentery, and as a general bitter tonic for appetite.
Plant Part Used: Stem (wood)
Preparation: A small piece of the stem is boiled in water until the water turns yellow. This decoction is taken in small doses (2–3 teaspoons) twice daily for diabetes and fever. Externally, the paste is applied to wounds and skin infections.
Precautions/Limitations: May cause digestive discomfort in high doses due to its bitterness; people on diabetes medication should monitor blood sugar due to its blood-sugar-lowering effect; not a substitute for confirmed antimalarial treatment.
Scientific Evidence Level: Traditional use; antidiabetic, antimicrobial and wound-healing activity shown in animal and laboratory studies only; the plant is endangered, so use is restricted.
Evidence by Use: Diabetes, bacterial infections, wound healing: reported in animal and in vitro studies; human trials of the whole plant were not found. Its constituent berberine has separate human data, which should not be attributed to the plant itself. Fever, jaundice, skin diseases, peptic ulcers, dysentery: traditional use.

Name (English): Katuwelbatu / Devil's Fig
Name (Sinhala): කටුවැල්බටු / Katuwelbatu
Scientific Name: Solanum virginianum
Family Name: Solanaceae
Medicinal Usage: Katuwelbatu is extensively used for respiratory diseases like asthma, chronic cough, and bronchitis. It also provides relief for toothache, chest pain, and fever. The plant has expectorant, anti-asthmatic, and anti-inflammatory properties.
Plant Part Used: Whole plant (especially fruits and roots)
Preparation: The dried fruits are smoked like tobacco to relieve asthma symptoms. A decoction of the root is given for chronic cough and bronchitis. For toothache, a small piece of the root is placed on the painful tooth. The whole plant decoction is used to reduce fever.
Precautions/Limitations: Smoking dried fruits for asthma relief is a traditional practice without strong clinical evidence and may harm respiratory health; this should not replace prescribed asthma inhalers or medication; the plant belongs to the nightshade family, so caution is advised with prolonged use.
Scientific Evidence Level: Traditional/folk use; limited formal research.
